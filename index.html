    How I think about agents

    The way I've found this works is basically the way a good team already runs. A capable model as the manager, cheaper models doing the legwork, and a person at the top holding the accountability. Reviewing everything at the end doesn't hold up. After twenty correct outputs, review turns into a reflex and the twenty-first error sails through. So the system brings the judgement calls forward as they come up, and remembers the answer.

    01 Building

    Four systems, designed and shipped end to end.

    Oracle, then Vedas 2025 to now in production

    An AI agent team for an accountancy firm.

    Fifteen stalled draft agents became one router in front of eighteen specialist lanes. Staff brief it like a junior and it routes itself. Now being rebuilt as the firm's own product on a custom harness, with the working rules enforced in code: the manager model can't do the juniors' legwork, memory saves need the user's word-for-word approval, and anything leaving the system needs human sign-off.

    What it actually does

    The VAT return auditor runs a tab-by-tab audit of Xero VAT exports against HMRC rules. The accounts-preparation agent runs a three-way reconciliation, from bank to ledger to trial balance, and raises a suspense report for anything unresolved. The client-onboarding agent builds a client's KYC and permanent file through a guided interview, auto-filling from firm records and Companies House. Job-card automation fills job-setup templates from prior-year data. Alfred, an onboarding and support agent that routes staff to the right tool, was the single biggest adoption win in staff testing.

    Two design rules that came out of incidents

    An overloaded combined agent hung under real data load. Root-caused to turn-size overload, split into two purpose-bound agents joined by a shared handover artefact. The rule that stuck: separate by function, bound each turn. And every agent drafts for staff review; nothing auto-sends or auto-files.

    Beau Dojo identity graph 90-day half-life

    beau.quest Jan to Feb 2026 shipped to beta

    A matching app that watches how you react, instead of asking who you are.

    Users play through AI-generated social simulations and react instinctively to moments that reveal who they really are, while a weighted identity graph learns their personality over time. Built solo through to a working beta with real users, then wound down once it had taught me what it was for.

    The thinking behind it

    Show, don't tell

    People reveal who they are through instinctive reactions, not self-reported bios. The "Dojo" simulation embeds abstract psychological dimensions in naturalistic scenarios. Users never see the underlying model.

    Identity as a living graph

    Traits, values, interests and vices sit on weighted spectra in a Neo4j graph, decaying over a 90-day half-life so the system always reflects who a user is now. Matching runs through a three-stage funnel that uses attachment-theory heuristics.

    Engagement by design

    Profiles unlock progressively based on conversation quality ("Fog of War"). A credit economy ties real AI costs to user-facing energy, creating a natural usage cadence without runaway spend.

    gonzo.guide 2026 paused, relaunch planned

    A travel engine that finds the places guidebooks don't know about.

    Six personas argue over the answer, the model searches in each city's own language, and every place is checked against Google Maps before it's allowed to be recommended. Born at the Cerebral Valley "Zero to Agent" hackathon with Google DeepMind and Vercel, then expanded across Telegram, Discord and web.

    The thinking behind it

    Six lenses, one debate

    Users pick from six AI personas: Local, Plot Twist, Foodie, Hangover, Bougie, Budget. Each one surfaces different places. "Council Mode" pits two or three personas against each other in a multi-turn debate, and a synthesis call picks the winner.

    Grounded, not hallucinated

    Every recommendation is verified real and open via Google Maps grounding before it's shown. The model searches in each city's own language to surface what locals actually discuss, and adjusts for the weather in real time.

    Natter Android fully on-device three models

    natter-releases September 2026 early build out

    A voice journal that writes the entry for you, on the phone, with nothing sent anywhere.

    You talk about your day; a small toad asks up to three questions and writes the page from your own sentences. Behind it, a notebook of the people, places and stories in your life builds itself. Speech, writing and a small clerk model all run on the handset, so it works in airplane mode.

    How it was built

    Three models, three jobs

    Parakeet hears, Gemma asks and writes, and a 35 MB Cactus Needle model acts as a clerk for spoken shortcuts. Version 1.5 started with a measured gate: 200 fixture passages on the PC and on the phone, the small model against the existing code and the large one. It won one job of five, so that is the one it got. The rest stayed where the numbers said.

    Stated, not guessed

    The writer only groups the user's own sentences; it cannot invent, soften or drop a fact. Notebook pages hold dated lines from what was said, never a character summary. "Ask me about your days" answers only from sources it can show as chips, and says plainly when it has nothing written down.

    Built by an agent team

    Planned and verified by Claude as orchestrator, built by Codex as the worker, each milestone gated by one unattended script driving the real phone. Two releases in two days, the second installed in place over the first with the journal intact.

    02 AI

    A year of putting agents into real work, one step at a time.

    The whole arc, including the bits that broke. It's written at the level I'd talk about it in an interview: the decisions and the failures, not the recipe.

    The arc June 2026 to now

    Six steps from fifteen stalled chatbots to a multi-tenant product. Scroll through them. The diagram on the left shows what the system looked like at each step.

    01 June 2026

    Fifteen agents, no code

    I joined a fifteen-person accountancy firm as the only AI person. A partner had already built fifteen draft chatbots on a no-code builder. Overlapping, untested, and half of them pointed at Google Drive in a Microsoft firm. I treated them as signal for what he wanted, never as a spec.

    Models and tools

    The firm's no-code agent builder on the model that came with it. I stayed on it because the partner had, and because switching platforms before I understood the work would've been guessing.

    The trade-off

    Speed to a first draft against having anything testable. Fifteen agents in a week is fast. Fifteen agents nobody can pick between is nothing.

    How I checked it worked

    Staff testing. The test wasn't accuracy yet, it was whether a person could tell which agent to open. Mostly they couldn't.

    What broke

    A combined agent that did categorisation and analytical review in one turn hung under real transaction volume. Splitting it by function, joined by a handover file, fixed it. That became a standing rule: separate by function, bound each turn.

    What I'd do differently

    Nothing much here. Reading the fifteen as intent rather than throwing them out is the reason the next step landed with the partner.

    02 July 2026

    Six agents and a front desk

    Fifteen became six with proper jobs: a regulatory researcher grounded in curated copies of the official sources, a VAT auditor, accounts prep, analytical review, a cash flow forecaster. Staff still couldn't pick. So the sixth agent's only job was to ask what you needed, choose the specialist, and write the prompt for you. Biggest adoption win of the year, for almost no build.

    Models and tools

    Same builder. The change was structure, not model. Search couldn't be locked to one domain, so the researcher read from curated copies instead of the open web.

    The trade-off

    One extra hop for the user against adoption. The front desk adds a turn to every request. It also meant people used the thing.

    How I checked it worked

    The partner's test runs of one workflow took sixteen minutes. Mine took four. That gap was the evaluation, and it took a week to explain.

    What broke

    Two causes for the sixteen minutes. An instruction asked the document connector for something it couldn't return, so the model went hunting and hit two five-minute timeouts. And the partner's personal system prompt, which just said hello and his name, derailed routing. Rule since: route the work first, personalise after, and never give the model an instruction the tool can't satisfy.

    What I'd do differently

    Put the front desk in on day one. I built specialists first because that's what looked like progress.

    03 Late July 2026

    Moving onto Claude

    We moved platforms because the new one could write real Excel and Word files from a sandbox that could see the uploads. Six agents became one router with seventeen lanes behind it, then the lanes became forty-odd markdown skills, read-only and provisioned to the whole firm. Integrations became MCP servers in their own repo. Built portable on purpose.

    Models and tools

    Claude with Projects and Skills. Skills as plain markdown, so they'd survive the next platform move. Integrations behind MCP, so the harness could change without the infrastructure moving. Both of those paid off twice within two months.

    The trade-off

    Platform lock-in against output quality. The sandbox was the reason to move. Keeping everything as files was the hedge against needing to move again.

    How I checked it worked

    In July we ran it head to head against the firm's best accounts prep staff on live jobs. About 80% of the work in about a quarter of the time, figures matching. Internal observation, not an audited study. The 80% is a cap I chose, not a ceiling it hit: after twenty correct outputs a reviewer stops reading the twenty-first.

    What broke

    A wrong column sum from a model looks identical to a right one. So the rule flipped: the model reads and judges, code adds up. Every figure in a deliverable now comes from a deterministic calculator the model can only call.

    What I'd do differently

    Move the arithmetic into code from the start. It took a wrong sum that looked right to convince me.

    04 August 2026

    Two harnesses that couldn't say no

    The next build, Vedas, was the same system rebuilt as the firm's own product on infrastructure it controls. It ran on two commercial agent harnesses in turn. Both worked. Neither could enforce the rules that mattered, because every rule was a sentence in a prompt, and I watched each one break.

    Models and tools

    An expensive model as the manager, a cheap one as the workers, wired through the harnesses' own delegation. Model slots through OpenRouter so the model behind each slot could swap by config.

    The trade-off

    A harness someone else maintains against one I'd have to. I stayed on commercial harnesses as long as the rules held. They didn't.

    How I checked it worked

    Telemetry. Sessions where the manager did all the mechanical extraction itself, at roughly eleven times the worker's price, because the document was right there in context and "please delegate" is a request.

    What broke

    A config slip routed the cheap model's work to the expensive model with nothing in any log saying so. Most expensive bug in the project. And once a bank statement was extracted and confirmed, its raw text sat in context until the harness compacted wholesale, throwing away the month-two note that mattered in month nine.

    What I'd do differently

    Ask "which of these rules has to be code" on day one. Three platforms in three months because the rules lived in prompts.

    05 August 2026

    A harness of my own, on pi

    I moved the system onto pi, a minimal open-source harness that extends in TypeScript, and wrote the rules as code that runs around the model. Four of them: the manager can't open source documents, so delegation is structural. Workers return a fixed summary and their transcript is dropped. Nothing is remembered until the user approves the exact wording. And once a figure table is confirmed, the raw document leaves the model's memory by decision rather than by overflow.

    Models and tools

    Three model slots set by environment variable, never chosen by the model: big for the manager and the cold review, small for conversion and extraction, vision only for pages the converter can't parse. Routing is static by task type because models are bad at judging difficulty before trying and good at recognising failure after. So the table is fixed and the dynamic rule is escalation. Small slot pinned to a dated version, because practice work may need explaining months later.

    The trade-off

    Cost: the manager is about eleven times the worker's input price, so it physically can't read the documents folder. Latency: extraction runs in parallel, computation never does, because each month's closing feeds the next month's opening. Quality: a low-confidence extraction triggers a second blind read, compared row by row, and every disagreement goes to a person. The tool can't average or pick a side. Reliability: a stage machine with teeth. You can't draft while any conversion lacks a confirmed table, and the cold review only runs on a finished draft.

    How I checked it worked

    First full run on 11 August: a real sole-trader year, bank tie-out to the penny, and the cold review caught a real figure error before any human saw it. That's one run, and I say so. What I don't have is a regression suite I can re-run when a model changes.

    What broke

    The practice system's API silently discards filters it doesn't recognise. One wrong parameter name returned the whole firm's jobs, HTTP 200, no error, plausible data about the wrong clients. Every response is now checked row by row against the scope asked for, and one foreign row fails the call. Separately, a blanket "re-run failures on the big model" rule sent thirteen document conversions to the expensive slot when the real fault was a cap in the dispatch layer. Keep the error text; the remedy differs per cause.

    What I'd do differently

    pi is young and its extension API moves, so every extension will need re-fitting. I chose it anyway because the alternative was a harness too big to hold the rules. I'd make the same call, and I'd budget for the re-fits.

    06 September 2026

    Vedas Cloud

    Last week I wrapped the harness in a multi-tenant web app. A practice signs in, connects its practice system read-only, and every job runs its own harness process in a worker with metered usage. The firm I built it for is tenant zero. It's days old, one real job has run end to end, and nobody has paid. I'm listing it because it's where the arc ends today, not because it's a result.

    Models and tools

    Next.js, a worker service on Railway, Postgres and storage on Supabase, Better Auth with organisations, Stripe for prepaid credit, OpenRouter with every request routed only to providers that keep nothing. The harness moved in unchanged. Most of the week was build tooling and agent orchestration on my side.

    The trade-off

    Privacy against availability. Zero-retention routing with fallbacks off is the strictest setting, and it's also the one that fails when a single provider has a bad hour. Three design rules shaped the rest: connectors are read-only by construction so nobody changes how they work. Doctrine is one static file for every practice and the runner injects what this practice actually has, so the model never pretends to have a tool it lacks. Templates are each firm's own, and none ship in the product.

    How I checked it worked

    One end-to-end job on a real tenant, and a browser walkthrough of every screen. That's the honest extent of it.

    What broke

    The first live job failed in twelve seconds with "model turn failed". Zero-retention routing with fallbacks off had landed on one upstream provider that was rate limited, and the request failed closed. Fix: allow fallbacks inside the zero-retention pool only, and record which provider served each request so the routing is auditable. Kept the fail-closed guarantee for when no zero-retention provider exists.

    What I'd do differently

    Build the evaluation harness before the product. The July benchmark is a one-off observation. Now that the default model has changed and the system is multi-tenant, a fixture set I can re-run on every model change is the most important thing I haven't built.

    Ninety percent of what the firm needed I built at the harness level and taught people to run. The last ten percent I built myself, and that's how I know where the line is.

    Day to day how I actually work

    I build through agents rather than by typing code. This section was made the same way: I wrote the copy and the spec, a worker built it, and I checked the result.

    1. Obsidian

      the second brain

      Every project, decision and conversation lands in one vault, as plain markdown in git. Agents read it before they start and file into it when they finish, so nothing depends on my memory or theirs.

    2. Claude Code and T3 Code

      the manager

      Planning, decomposition, briefs, design, and checking what comes back. Same shape as the accounts system: the expensive model routes and reviews, it doesn't do the legwork.

    3. Codex

      the workers

      Scoped implementation slices, each in its own git worktree, running in parallel. A brief goes in, a report comes out, and I read the diff before I believe it.

    4. Skills and MCP

      the hands

      Rules and methods as markdown skills, integrations as MCP servers in their own repo. Draft-never-send is skill law: nothing goes out without a yes from me.

    Writing

    I'm dyslexic. The thinking is fine; when I talk the ideas come out in order. What goes missing is on the page: one word won't come and the sentence stalls behind it. So anything that has to be mine, I write with a method. I talk it out first. The AI hands back a skeleton of numbered prompts, never sentences. I write each one, and when a word blocks I put its meaning in square brackets and keep going. Then I get three options per bracket and pick. It counts words, names the clause to cut, and scans for the tells. It never writes a sentence.

    Everything else in my name, including most of this site, is drafted with AI assistance, which I use as a dyslexia adjustment first provided through my Disabled Students' Allowance. Every fact on it comes from my own maintained record and has been checked by me.

    Where it's going four things I believe, each with the incident behind it

    1. 01

      The model is the cheap part.

      The whole accounts system now runs on a flash-class open-weight model by default, swapped in with a one-line config change. A Sonnet-class model is plenty for professional back-office work, and open-weight models are there. What's durable is the method, the harness, and the corrections a firm has accumulated. The last three months of the project were all harness and zero model.

    2. 02

      A rule that matters has to be code.

      Every prompt-level rule I've written has eventually been ignored, and the cost showed up in telemetry, not in a log. The version that holds is the one where the manager can't open the file, the worker can't compute, and nobody can edit the instructions from inside a session.

    3. 03

      Memory is the permanent file.

      The industry treats memory as a feature. Accountancy invented it a century ago. The interesting engineering is making it reviewable: nothing written until the person confirms the wording, dated, with provenance, append only. A wrong memory compounds silently, quarter after quarter.

    4. 04

      The human belongs in the middle, not at the end.

      Review at the end degrades after twenty correct outputs. The system stops and asks when the ambiguity appears, and the answer steers the job. That's why the work is capped at 80% on purpose, and why the partner reviews working papers, never conclusions.
